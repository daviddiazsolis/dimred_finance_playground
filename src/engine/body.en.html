A loyalty program and 400 normal purchases

Before the 29 columns of a real dataset, a picture with two. With every purchase the card earns points roughly proportional to the amount. Each gray dot is a normal purchase, with amount and points standardized (mean 0, standard deviation 1). Move the correlation: with a strong relationship, normal purchases live almost on a line.

That green line is the first principal component: the direction in which the data vary the most. Principal component analysis (PCA; Pearson, 1901; Hotelling, 1933) finds it without being told that amount and points go together. The second component is the perpendicular one, and with a correlation of 0.9 it explains only about 5% of the variation.

Project, reconstruct and measure the error

If we keep only the first component, each purchase is summarized by one number: its position along the line (its score). From that number the purchase can be reconstructed: it is the closest point on the line (the hollow circle). The squared distance between the purchase and its reconstruction, the dotted line, is the reconstruction error.

A is a huge purchase with the points it should earn: extreme, but it fits the pattern, and its error is almost zero. B is a purchase with a moderate amount and moderate points, but it does not fit: it has more points than its amount should earn. Move B with the controls and watch its error against the 99th percentile of the error of normal purchases.

That is the whole idea behind the fraud detector of this module: it does not look for large transactions, it looks for transactions that break the relationships normal ones obey. In fraud this is exactly what is needed, because each piece of data, on its own, often looks innocent.

The minimum math

With \(p\) standardized variables, PCA looks for the eigenvectors of the covariance matrix \(S\):

Eigendecomposition\[ S\, w_j = \lambda_j\, w_j, \qquad \lambda_1 \ge \lambda_2 \ge \dots \ge \lambda_p \]

\(w_j\) is direction \(j\), a vector of length 1 whose elements are the loadings: how much each variable weighs in that direction. \(\lambda_j\) is the variance of the data along \(w_j\), and the explained variance is \(\lambda_j / \sum_i \lambda_i\). Keeping the first \(k\) directions (the columns of \(W_k\)), an observation \(x\) is summarized and reconstructed as:

Scores and reconstruction\[ z = W_k^{\top}(x - \mu), \qquad \hat{x} = \mu + W_k\, z \]

\(z\) are the \(k\) scores and \(\hat{x}\) the reconstruction. The reconstruction error (the \(Q\) statistic or SPE of Jackson and Mudholkar, 1979) is

Reconstruction error\[ e(x) = \lVert x - \hat{x} \rVert^2 = \sum_{j=k+1}^{p} z_j^2 \]

The last equality is the key to the whole module: the error is the sum of the squared scores on the components we discard. A transaction has a large error if it moves a lot in directions where normal ones barely move. In the picture of this tab, with the correlation you chose:

284,807 transactions, 492 frauds, and the label arrives late

The Credit Card Fraud Detection dataset (Dal Pozzolo et al., 2015) holds every card transaction of a European issuer over two days in 2013. Fraud is 0.17%: a model that says nothing is fraud is right 99.83% of the time, so we measure with PR-AUC (area under the precision-recall curve) and with precision@100: of the 100 most suspicious transactions, how many are fraud.

In fraud, the label arrives when the customer complains, days or weeks later, and new fraud has no label at all. That is why anomaly detection is used: learn what normal looks like and flag whatever does not fit. The detector of this module is the PCA of tab 01: it is fitted on training without looking at the label, and the reconstruction error is the suspicion score.

For confidentiality, the issuer released 28 columns V1 to V28 that are already principal components of the original variables; only Time and Amount are in their own scale. We use V1 to V28 and Amount (29 columns) and leave out Time, which is a clock rather than a feature. Stratified random split, 67% and 33%.

Explained variance: no handful of components explains it all here

In textbooks, two or three components explain almost everything. Here each one explains between 3% and 4%, except the first (7%) and the last (0.14%), and reaching 95% takes 27 of 29. The reason: V1 to V28 are already principal components, they are uncorrelated, and once standardized each contributes the same. PCA only finds structure in the relationship between Amount and a few Vs.

For an anomaly detector this is fine. We are not trying to summarize in a few columns: we are looking for the few directions in which normal transactions barely move, which are the last ones.

The loadings: what each component is

components_ has one row per component and one column per variable. Compare the three:

  • PC1: loading 0.71 on the amount and opposite-sign loadings on V2 and V5: the "amount" direction, with the Vs that go with it in a normal purchase.
  • PC29: the same variables with the signs flipped. It is "an amount that does not match its Vs", the analogue of component 2 in the loyalty-points picture.
  • PC28: positive loadings on V17, V12, V14, V10, V16 and V7. Keep this list in mind: it does almost all the work.

How many components to keep?

With PCA(n_components=0.95) scikit-learn picks the smallest \(k\) that reaches 95% of the variance, without looking at any label: here, \(k = 27\). The error is made of the two discarded components, PC28 and PC29. Move \(k\) and watch the PR-AUC on testing (this does use the labels, so it is a diagnostic we could not run in practice).

With \(k = 28\) the detector collapses: PC28 is kept and fraud is reconstructed well. The 95% rule lands on the best value, partly by luck. With anomaly detectors you have to look at which components are discarded, not only at how much variance is kept.

The threshold, without labels

Raising alerts takes a threshold, and without labels we cannot search for the one that maximizes recall. What we do know is how many alerts the team can review. The rule: flag whatever exceeds a high percentile of the training error; with the 99.8th percentile about 0.2% of transactions are flagged, close to the fraud rate. Move the percentile:

The two distributions are far apart, but the right tail of normal transactions reaches as far as the frauds: an anomaly detector finds what is rare, and not everything rare is fraud. And almost one fraud in five has an error as low as a normal purchase: no threshold will find them with this score.

Why does it work? A clue in how the data were anonymized

Almost all the detection is done by PC28, the direction of V17, V12, V14, V10: exactly the variables where frauds take very negative values all at once. Why did PCA send it to the end of the list? Compare the correlations among those variables in all transactions and in normal ones only:

All transactions
Normal ones only

In the full dataset the correlation is exactly zero: the Vs are principal components computed by the issuer on all transactions, frauds included. Among normal transactions negative correlations appear, which the frauds (all extreme in the same direction) offset. As a result, among normal ones that combination varies less than it should, PCA discards it, and by discarding it turns it into the detector.

A double lesson: PCA finds on its own the direction where normal transactions do not move; but part of that direction comes from how this dataset was anonymized. With raw data the result would be more modest. Always ask why a detector works, not only how much.

How much do we lose by not using labels?

The comparison with the supervised models from the classification notebooks, with the same columns and splits: a logistic regression, a regularized gradient boosting and a logistic regression that knows only 20 frauds. Switch the split:

  • With all the labels, supervised wins, gradient boosting above all.
  • In the future the gap narrows: in precision@100, clean PCA beats the logistic regression trained on all the labels.
  • With few labels, it depends: the logistic with 20 frauds ties PCA on the random split, but falls below it in the future. Few labels generalize poorly to fraud that changes.
  • Contaminated PCA is the only one that collapses: its problem is not the algorithm but what we told it was normal.

Five yields that move together

BCU and BTU bonds pay in UF, Chile's inflation-indexed unit, so their yield is a real yield. The Central Bank publishes every day the yield of a representative bond at 2, 5, 10, 20 and 30 years: the yield curve. A pension fund, a life insurer or a bank treasury holds bonds at all of those maturities, and the value of the portfolio depends on five yields at once. Move the week (2008 to 2021) and watch the curve: it shifts up and down as a whole, steepens and flattens, bends. In 2020 the short maturities reached negative real yields.

Litterman and Scheinkman (1991) showed that the curve almost always moves in three ways: level, slope and curvature. PCA finds them without being told.

Preparing the data: gaps, frequency, levels or changes

Gaps. Each maturity is the yield of the bond closest to that maturity, and there are days and years with no benchmark bond. Percentage of days with data, by maturity (rows, in years) and year (columns):

Three decisions: a 2008 to 2021 window, when all five maturities exist; weekly frequency (the last yield of each week), which completes weeks without inventing data; and never backfilling (bfill), which copies a future value into the past and leaves a flat yield that never existed.

Levels or changes. On levels, the first component seems to explain almost everything. But a PCA on the levels of five independent random walks also finds a "dominant component": trending series look alike by chance, the PCA version of Granger and Newbold's (1974) spurious regression. That is why we work with weekly changes in basis points.

Standardizing. The 2-year maturity moves 20 basis points per week and the long ones 10. Without standardizing, the first component would be almost "the 2-year yield"; standardized, it is a clean level.

The three shapes, found by PCA

Each week, the move of the curve is a mix of three fixed shapes (the loadings \(w_1, w_2, w_3\)), and what changes is how much of each there was (the factors \(f_{j,t}\)):

Three-factor model\[ x_t \approx f_{1,t}\, w_1 + f_{2,t}\, w_2 + f_{3,t}\, w_3 \]

Level (70%): similar loadings with the same sign, the whole curve together. Slope (16%): negative at the short end, positive at the long end; when it rises, the curve steepens. Curvature (8%): the 5 to 10-year segment against both ends. Together, 94% of weekly moves. The names are checked: the level factor has a correlation of 0.98 with the average change, the slope factor 0.96 with the 30 minus 2-year spread, and the curvature factor 0.87 with the butterfly.

How good is it? Error in basis points, in and out of sample

The error is measured in business units: if I describe each week with \(k\) factors and reconstruct the five yields (with the inverse_transform of PCA and of the scaler), by how many basis points am I off? Out of sample, the shapes are estimated on 2008 to 2014 and used on 2015 to 2021, a period that includes the 2019 social unrest and the pandemic.

With the level alone, the 2-year maturity is left almost unexplained; the slope fixes it. With three factors the error is a few basis points per week, and out of sample it barely rises: the shapes of 2008 to 2014 and of 2015 to 2021 have correlations of 0.96, 0.96 and 0.91.

A one-standard-deviation shock to factor \(j\) moves the curve by \(\sqrt{\lambda_j}\,w_j\) (in standardized units); multiplying by the standard deviation of each maturity takes us back to basis points. A network trained to return what it receives

An autoencoder (Hinton and Salakhutdinov, 2006) is a neural network with a seemingly absurd task: reproduce its own input. The trick is the bottleneck in the middle, which forces the information to be compressed.

Encode, decode and measure the error\[ h = f(x), \qquad \hat{x} = g(h), \qquad \text{loss} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lVert x_i - \hat{x}_i \rVert^2 \]

\(f\) is the encoder, \(h\) the 26-number code (embedding) and \(g\) the decoder. Each dense layer computes \(\sigma(A\,u + b)\); with the ReLU activation, \(\sigma(t) = \max(0, t)\), the network can fold space and follow curved surfaces that PCA cannot. The fraud score is the same reconstruction error as in PCA. Same honest setting as tab 03: split by time and training on normal transactions only.

A linear autoencoder is a PCA

Baldi and Hornik (1989) showed that an autoencoder without activations, trained with squared error, reconstructs exactly like PCA with the same number of components. In Keras it takes four lines: Input(29), Dense(26), Dense(29), compile(loss="mse"), and it is trained with fit(X, X): the same matrix as input and as target. The result on the data:

Practically the same detector. The difference is procedural: PCA finds the exact solution with algebra in an instant; the network searches for it with gradient descent over several epochs and gets close. What an autoencoder adds lies in the nonlinear activations.

Nonlinear: it reconstructs better, it does not detect better

The version with a hidden layer of 64 ReLU neurons on each side of the code, trained with three seeds (the initial weights are random):

The nonlinear autoencoder reconstructs normal transactions much better and ranks the whole set somewhat better (its ROC AUC is higher), but where it matters, in PR-AUC and in the top alerts, it depends on the seed and on average falls below PCA. The detector does not need to reconstruct everything well: it needs to reconstruct normal transactions well and fraud badly. A more flexible network also learns to reconstruct combinations it never saw, and the contrast shrinks.

The usual lesson in another costume: more complexity is not more information. In this dataset the pattern that separates fraud is almost linear, and the linear model captures it more reliably.

Looking inside the autoencoder's code

The encoder compresses each transaction into 26 numbers. To see them we have to go down to 2 dimensions. PCA to 2D is linear and preserves variance; t-SNE (van der Maaten and Hinton, 2008) looks for a drawing where close neighbors stay neighbors; UMAP (McInnes, Healy and Melville, 2018) does the same with different math, faster, and can apply the map to new data. Sample: 1,500 normal transactions and the 108 testing frauds.

In PCA to 2D the frauds stick out as a tail from the cloud. t-SNE and UMAP open the cloud and show groups of their own for frauds, and another share of the frauds mixed in with normal transactions: the ones no reconstruction detector will find.

What you cannot conclude from a t-SNE

These are 3,000 points of pure Gaussian noise in 26 dimensions: there are no groups. Look at what each method draws:

  • The distances between groups and the sizes of groups in a t-SNE mean nothing.
  • The parameters (the perplexity, the seed) change the drawing.
  • t-SNE can invent structure where there is none. A group in t-SNE or UMAP is a hypothesis; it is confirmed with something else.

Review questions

Try to answer before opening each answer.

1. A transaction has the highest amount in the whole dataset and yet its reconstruction error is almost zero. How is that possible?

The error measures how far the transaction departs from the directions PCA kept, not how large it is. If the high amount comes with the other variables as in a normal purchase (like point A in tab 01), the transaction falls on the subspace of normal behavior and is reconstructed well.

2. Why is the reconstruction error equal to the sum of the squared scores on the discarded components?

Because PCA directions are perpendicular to each other and have length 1. A centered observation is written exactly as the sum of its scores times its directions; reconstructing with \(k\) directions, what is missing is the part along directions \(k+1\) to \(p\), and its squared length is the sum of those squared scores (Pythagoras in \(p\) dimensions).

3. In the fraud dataset, why does the 95% rule keep 27 of 29 components, when textbooks need two or three?

Because the variables V1 to V28 are already principal components: they are uncorrelated, and once standardized each contributes the same variance. Only the relationship of the amount with some Vs gives structure. Without correlations there is nothing to summarize.

4. PCA fitted on all of training worked with the random split and failed with the time split. What changed?

In the time-based training set, the first-day frauds were so extreme along the fraud direction that PCA ranked it as component 2 and kept it: it learned fraud as part of normal behavior. That is contamination of the training set. It is fixed by fitting only on transactions believed to be normal.

5. Why is it not a good idea to apply PCA to yield levels?

Because levels have long trends (they are not stationary), and trending series look correlated even when they are unrelated: PCA on the levels of five independent random walks finds a component that explains more than half the variance. With changes, the illusion disappears.

6. On the UF curve, why standardize the changes if they are all in basis points?

Because the 2-year maturity is twice as volatile as the long ones and, without standardizing, takes over the first component: the "level" would be almost the 2-year yield. Standardized, the first component is a clean level and the shapes are stable across periods. The choice depends on which maturities matter to whoever uses the model.

7. What is special about an autoencoder without activations?

It is a PCA: with squared loss, its best solution reconstructs like PCA with the same number of components (Baldi and Hornik, 1989). For an autoencoder to add something, it needs nonlinear activations.

8. The nonlinear autoencoder reconstructs better than PCA. Why does it not detect better?

Because detecting requires reconstructing normal transactions well and anomalies badly. A more flexible network also learns to reconstruct rare combinations, including part of the fraud, and the contrast between normal transactions and frauds shrinks. Its result also depends on the seed.

9. A t-SNE of a bank's customers shows five well-separated groups. What can you conclude?

Nothing yet. t-SNE can draw groups in data with no structure, and the distances and sizes of its groups mean nothing. The groups are a hypothesis: confirm them with another tool (clustering on the original data, an external variable, another sample).

10. If you have plenty of reliable fraud labels, what is an unsupervised detector for?

For fraud that has no labels yet (a new attack), for the first days of a system and as a safety net: in the future, the supervised model trained with few labels drops more than clean PCA. In practice fraud teams run both.
